A Proclamation for the Protection of Human Personhood

❦

Our First Priority

Protect the meaning of human personhood before it becomes confused.

We are concerned about a future in which machines may imitate human conversation, judgment, emotion and even the outward signs of consciousness so convincingly that society begins to confuse human consciousness and human personhood with the abilities of constructed artificial systems.

Human rights are the rights of human beings. Those protections must remain clear and secure for all people, regardless of citizenship, as debates emerge about what legal recognition or limited rights, if any, might someday be appropriate for robots and other artificial entities.

Animals, organizations and other entities may have particular legal protections or standing, but those are not automatically the same as the fundamental rights of human persons. We call for the careful preparation of federal and state laws, and consideration of constitutional clarification where needed, to preserve that distinction.

In plain language: Copyrights are for people, not robots. That is one familiar example of a much broader principle: being able to imitate human capabilities must not, by itself, blur the legal meaning of being human.

This is a proposal for public discussion and legislative preparation, not a claim that artificial systems currently possess human rights or that consciousness has a settled scientific definition.

Whereas the growing abilities of artificial intelligence and robotics may one day resemble human thought, speech, judgment and creativity, we affirm that resemblance alone must not erase the distinction between human beings and constructed entities.

We proclaim:

  1. Human rights belong to human beings. The dignity and fundamental rights of people must remain identifiable and protected, irrespective of technological progress.
  2. Consciousness and personhood must not be carelessly conflated. Future debates about artificial intelligence must distinguish apparent capabilities, questions of consciousness and legal personhood rather than treating them as the same thing.
  3. Different entities may have different legal standing. Protections considered for animals, corporations or artificial systems must be discussed on their own terms, without silently redefining human rights.
  4. Copyrights are for people, not robots. Our shorthand is that human creative authorship matters. Copyright is one example of why law should retain clear distinctions between people and technology.
  5. Prepare safeguards now. We ask lawmakers, scholars and the public to examine federal and state measures—and constitutional clarifications where appropriate—before future confusion weakens the protections owed to people.

Our common purpose: We do not reject technology or claim to have solved every philosophical question. We seek clarity so that legal rights designed to protect human beings do not become confused with other forms of legal recognition.
